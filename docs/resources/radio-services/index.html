Radio Services

There are many different types of radios, each designed for specific uses, such as talking with family, coordinating work, or communicating during emergencies. In the United States, the Federal Communications Commission (FCC) sets rules for how these radios can be used, including which frequencies are allowed and whether a license is required.

This guide explains the most common radio services, what they are used for, and their frequencies. Remember, just because you can listen to a frequency does not mean you are allowed to transmit on it.

Radio Services​

Understanding Radio Licenses: Some radio services can be used without a personal FCC license, while others require one. Certain services are limited to authorized users or organizations. Regardless of licensing requirements, you must use equipment and frequencies permitted for that service.

Personal & Community Communications

FRS

Family Radio Service

462–467 MHz · UHF
No Individual License
Channels22
Maximum Power2 W ERP
RepeatersNot permitted

Family Radio Service (FRS) provides short-range communication for families, outdoor activities, and small groups. It uses 22 shared channels and does not require an individual FCC license. FRS radios have built-in antennas and cannot use repeaters.

MURS

Multi-Use Radio Service

151–154 MHz · VHF

RepeatersNot permitted

Multi-Use Radio Service (MURS) provides short-range communication for personal, recreational, and business activities. It uses five VHF channels and does not require an individual FCC license. External antennas are permitted, but repeaters are not allowed.

Citizens Band (CB) radio provides communication for travelers, truck drivers, outdoor enthusiasts, and businesses. It uses 40 shared channels and does not require an individual FCC license. CB radios support AM, FM, and single-sideband communication without repeaters.

GMRS

General Mobile Radio Service

462–467 MHz · UHF
FCC License Required
Channels22 + 8 repeater pairs
Maximum PowerUp to 50 W Output
RepeatersPermitted

General Mobile Radio Service (GMRS) provides personal and family communications using handheld radios, mobile radios, and repeaters. An FCC license is required and generally covers eligible immediate family members. GMRS shares channels with FRS but permits higher-power equipment and repeaters for extended coverage.

HAM

Amateur Radio

Multiple HF, VHF, UHF and microwave bands
FCC License Required

Amateur radio allows licensed operators to communicate locally, across the country, and around the world. It is used for personal communication, technical experimentation, education, and volunteer public service, including emergency communications. Available frequencies and operating privileges depend on the operator’s FCC license class.

FCC Service Rules
LoRa

LoRa / Mesh Communications

Low-power digital communications · 902–928 MHz
Configuration Dependent

LoRa is a low-power radio technology used to send text messages, location information, and other small amounts of data over long distances. Systems such as Meshtastic and MeshCore use LoRa to communicate without cellular service or internet access. Although they can use similar equipment, they operate as separate networks with different messaging and routing methods.

LoRa / Mesh Communications Frequencies

LoRa is a radio technology, not a separate FCC radio service. The frequencies shown are Meshtastic examples and do not necessarily apply to MeshCore. Devices must use compatible radio settings to communicate. Meshtastic and MeshCore cannot directly exchange messages simply because they use the same LoRa hardware. All equipment and configurations must comply with applicable FCC regulations.

Meshtastic

Meshtastic lets compatible radios exchange messages and location information without cell service or internet access.

Network: A distributed mesh where participating devices can relay messages. Dedicated repeaters are not required.

Messaging: Group channels, direct messages, and location sharing.

Common uses: Outdoor recreation, position reporting, community communication, and experimentation.

Meshtastic Website

MeshCore

MeshCore lets compatible radios exchange messages directly or through a network of dedicated repeaters without cell service or internet access.

Network: User devices communicate directly or through repeaters that forward messages along network paths.

Messaging: Public channels, private messages, and optional room servers for stored messages.

Common uses: Community messaging, fixed repeater networks, group coordination, and experimentation.

MeshCore Website

Meshtastic and MeshCore use LoRa technology but are separate systems. Devices require compatible firmware and radio settings to communicate.

Commercial & Transportation

Business Band

Commercial Land Mobile Radio · FCC Part 90
FCC License Required

Business radio systems provide communication for companies, organizations, transportation services, and commercial operations. They allow employees to coordinate daily activities using frequencies authorized for their organization. FCC licensing requirements, equipment, and operating privileges depend on the specific system.

FCC Part 90 Rules

Marine Radio

156–162 MHz · Marine VHF
Authorization Varies

Marine radio provides communication between boats, ships, coastal stations, and maritime authorities. It is used for navigation, vessel coordination, safety information, and emergency communications. Marine VHF Channel 16 is the international distress, safety, and calling channel.

Aviation Radio

118–137 MHz · VHF Airband
Restricted / Authorized Use

Aviation radio provides communication between pilots, air traffic controllers, and airport personnel. It is used to coordinate aircraft movements, manage air traffic, share weather information, and support emergency communications. Most civilian aviation voice communications use AM radio in the VHF airband.

Railroad Radio

160–162 MHz · VHF
Restricted / Authorized Use

Railroad radio provides communication between train crews, dispatchers, and yard personnel. It is used to coordinate train movements, switching operations, track maintenance, and railroad safety. In Utah Valley, Union Pacific and other railroad operations use standardized AAR radio channels.

Wilderness Protocol

Amateur emergency monitoring practice
FCC License Required

The Wilderness Protocol is an amateur radio practice intended to improve the chances of making contact in remote areas. Operators are encouraged to periodically monitor established calling frequencies for stations needing assistance. Participation is voluntary and does not guarantee that someone will be listening during an emergency.

Comparing Radio Services​

The estimates below cover basic entry-level equipment in the United States. Range is highly variable and depends on terrain, antenna height, buildings, interference, equipment, and whether an existing repeater or mesh network is available. These are planning estimates, not guaranteed distances or price quotes.

Radio serviceApproximate cost of entryTypical direct range
FRS$20–$60 for a pair0.5–2 miles
GMRS$50–$150 per radio, plus FCC license1–5 miles
MURS$40–$100 per radio1–3 miles
CB$60–$200 for basic radio and antenna1–5 miles
LoRa / Mesh$25–$100 per node1–10+ miles between favorably placed nodes
Amateur Radio$30–$150 for an entry-level handheld, plus testing/licensing costs1–5 miles with an entry-level VHF/UHF handheld; repeaters and HF can extend coverage much farther

Costs are approximate U.S. equipment budgets, not recurring service fees. The ranges are broad illustrations for common setups, not statistical averages. GMRS repeater access and mesh relays can extend coverage substantially; amateur-radio range varies enormously by band and operating mode. Business and transportation services use specialized equipment and authorization, so a single consumer entry-cost estimate would be misleading.
